# Pitch: what dealer positioning adds to your expiry-day result

*For Prof. Piyush Pandey. From Shreyan Thanki, November 2026. Every result below is from **real NSE data**. The forecasting and dealer-gamma results use 2 January 2023 to 2 November 2026 (942 trading days, 200 Nifty expiries); the replication of your paper uses 20 October 2022 to 30 June 2024, and the event study starts in March 2019. Each result links to a document in this repository that shows the file it comes from. "Registered" below means the analysis plan was committed to git before its results were computed (no external registry, and the data were not new).*

## The idea in three sentences

Your 2025 paper with Chhimwal and V. Pandey shows that each index became more volatile and more heavily traded on its own expiry day after NSE separated the Nifty and Bank Nifty expiries. I tested one candidate explanation, option dealers' gamma hedging, with public NSE files, first by asking whether dealer positioning improves out-of-sample forecasts beyond your mcsGARCH model, and then by trying hard to break every in-sample link I found. The answer is more interesting than a yes or a no, and it gives a concrete plan for a paper.

## What I found

1. **Your "after" sample replicates almost exactly, and I think I found your "before" window, to within about a week.** With 360 minutes a day I get exactly your 21,239 Nifty returns and Table 1's extremes to the printed digit; mcsGARCH gives alpha 0.0498 and beta 0.9407 (printed 0.0494 and 0.9410). The stated "before" period (November 2023 to February 2024) has 82 trading days, not 64, and does not match. Two independent searches of every 64-day window against Table 1 put it one year earlier, starting between 14 and 23 November 2022 and ending between 10 and 21 February 2023, with both printed "before" maxima reproduced to every decimal. The first search, which scores the spread and the tails, picks **14 November 2022 to 10 February 2023**; a second, registered search, run blind to the first by a separate AI agent, also uses your printed means and picks **22 November 2022 to 20 February 2023**, one of only two windows that reproduce both means. My first question to you is which dates you used. ([REPLICATION_NSE.md](docs/REPLICATION_NSE.md), [P108_BLIND_WINDOW.md](docs/P108_BLIND_WINDOW.md))
2. **At short horizons your model can be improved, and most of the improvement can be written as a few plain fixes to mcsGARCH.** Your paper fits mcsGARCH but does not forecast with it, so I ran an out-of-sample contest on 792 Nifty test days. A hybrid that corrects mcsGARCH's forecast with a gradient-boosted model, using 41 inputs (recent realized volatility, the time of day, the calendar, India VIX and the previous evening's option chain), forecasts the next 15 and 30 minutes 4.4% and 6.6% better than mcsGARCH as your paper specifies it (registered, P116; QLIKE, a standard score for volatility forecasts that punishes forecasts that are too low; Diebold-Mariano p below 0.001). Rebuilt on your specification instead, with nothing else changed, the same recipe is 5.1% and 7.1% better than your model (registered, P118, averaged over five sets of random seeds; p below 0.001), which is the like-for-like comparison: the base and the benchmark are the same model. Two caveats come with these numbers. The hybrid used in the studies below corrects my own implementation of mcsGARCH, which adds a separate intraday curve for expiry days and smooths the curves; on Nifty that version forecasts slightly worse than yours (on Bank Nifty slightly better), against it the gain is 5.8% and 7.6%, and the studies below, run before I checked this, use it as the benchmark. The correction absorbs about half to three quarters of the difference between the two bases, and whether the hybrid built on yours is better than the one built on mine cannot be settled (0.65 and 0.25 points, inconclusive under the registered rule), except on expiry days, where my separate expiry-day curve costs about 2 points at 15 minutes. And plain gradient boosting, without mcsGARCH, cannot be told apart from the hybrid at any horizon (p 0.32 to 0.97), so what matters is learning from recent volatility and the time of day, not the hybrid's structure. Against my version, the gain survives a correction for having tried 31 models and retraining with 20 new sets of random seeds.
   * **Where the gain comes from** (registered, P106 and P110). Recent realized volatility carries about a third of the gain and the time of day about a quarter. On Nifty, removing the option chain or VIX changes the loss by less than 1% of mcsGARCH's (removing both together raises it by 0.6 points, not significant and not tested for equivalence), removing the calendar inputs slightly improves it, and a hybrid that sees only what mcsGARCH sees gets the whole gain (6.1% and 8.0% over my version). On Bank Nifty and Fin Nifty, with five sets of random seeds, the option chain and VIX are again each worth less than 1% of mcsGARCH's loss, the calendar inputs help a little, and the mcsGARCH-information hybrid gets most, not all, of the gain.
   * **Five plain fixes** (registered, P109). Written down before computing any result, the five fixes together make my version 6.8% and 8.3% better at 15 and 30 minutes, 118% and 108% of the hybrid's gain over it (intervals 85% to 167% and 80% to 132%), and the hybrid cannot be told apart from the fixed model (p 0.82 and 0.73); against your specification the fixed model is 5.5% and 7.3% better. Three fixes do most of it: giving the minute-by-minute part today's level so far, one calibration factor per time of day, and letting yesterday's close fade overnight; a HAR daily level helps only inside the combination, and leverage does not help. These shares are of the gain over my version, and part of the time-of-day fix, like part of the hybrid's correction, undoes my own smoothing around 15:00 (P116). The fixed model does not explain the last hour (13% of the hybrid's gain there), and on Bank Nifty and Fin Nifty, with nothing retuned, it recovers 97% to 136%. Because the fixes were chosen after seeing these days, this explains the gain rather than proving a better model; a one-time forward test on Nifty days from 4 November 2026 (P112) will test them against my version and against your specification.
   * **Replications** (registered). With nothing changed, the hybrid is 3.9% and 5.0% better than my version on 660 Bank Nifty test days (4.7% and 6.2% against your specification), and 4.9% and 6.1% better on 604 Fin Nifty test days (4.0% and 5.2%), although on Fin Nifty the last-hour gain (1.0%, p 0.30) and the expiry-day gain (Holm p 0.093) are not significant. Pooled over the three indices, allowing for them moving together, the gain over my version is 4.7% and 6.0%; this summarises evidence already seen rather than testing it anew, and the three indices count as about two independent replications, not three.
   * **Limits** (post hoc, after an AI referee report, except the last sentence). Against the best of four versions of my mcsGARCH also given India VIX or option-implied variance, the hybrid is better by 4.8% and 5.9%, but that edge appears only after November 2024 and does not survive the correction for all of the project's tests; on point estimates it appears then mostly because that benchmark lost its advantage (82% to 87% of the change), although the benchmark's own change is not significant. In the last hour an mcsGARCH given VIX comes within 1.7% of the hybrid's 6.4% gain, and under squared-error loss the models cannot be ranked. In money terms the gain is small: under a registered test, an investor who sizes a Nifty position by forecast volatility would pay only about 0.04% of wealth a year to switch from my version of mcsGARCH to the hybrid, and against an mcsGARCH given the implied variance the value is not significant.

3. **The expiry effect lives at the end of the day, and part of it is noise.** Since Nifty's expiry moved to Tuesday (September 2025), Tuesday's last hour carries 63% more variance than ordinary days on which no NSE or BSE index option expires (26% more over the whole day; this comparison is post hoc, after an AI referee report). Before November 2024 almost every day was some index's expiry day, so no clean comparison exists for that period. When Bank Nifty's expiry moved from Thursday to Wednesday in 2023, its end-of-day variance moved with it (+0.90 log points in the last 30 minutes, with no baseline needed). Two cautions: end-of-day one-minute returns on expiry days reverse quickly, a sign of bid-ask noise, and a noise-robust estimator keeps only a half to three quarters of each premium; and the event-study placebo tests rest on at most four independent six-month windows, so none of those results reaches 5% under an honest small-sample test. A synthetic-control study of the November 2024 cut in weekly options was too imprecise to tell. ([REAL_DATA_RESULTS.md](docs/REAL_DATA_RESULTS.md), R2; [EVENT_STUDY.md](docs/EVENT_STUDY.md); [REFEREE_RESPONSE_EXPIRY.md](docs/REFEREE_RESPONSE_EXPIRY.md); [SYNTHETIC_CONTROL.md](docs/SYNTHETIC_CONTROL.md))
4. **Dealer gamma from public files does not help forecasts, and the one in-sample pattern that held up is about Pro positioning, not gamma.** Dealer gamma exposure (GEX: how much option dealers must buy or sell to stay hedged when the index moves) adds nothing out of sample on Nifty under any of three sign rules (on Bank Nifty a small gain at the 09:15 forecast needs its own test, and it did not reappear on Fin Nifty). In sample, Nifty reverses into the close (about 3 bp per 100 bp move over the day), which already counts against the simplest hedging story, since dealers short all options would make the close continue the day's move. The reversal appears only after evenings when NSE's "Pro" participants were net short index options (equivalently, Pro-based GEX was negative: the two agree on 916 of 932 evenings, and weighting by gamma adds nothing). It survived block shuffles, timing placebos, random sign rules, a split-sample test and a Holm correction over the 78 headline tests (p = 0.016), but not a correction over the full family of 742 tests. The negative-Pro evenings come in long runs (95% of 2023 evenings, about one in five from 2025), so the honest sample is a few dozen episodes; with inference built for that, the pattern holds in the full sample and from 2025 but not inside 2023 to 2024. As a trade it earns about 4 bp before costs and about zero after them (break-even slippage about 0.55 bp per side). ([FALSIFICATION.md](docs/FALSIFICATION.md), [F2_ECONOMICS.md](docs/F2_ECONOMICS.md), [REFEREE_RESPONSE_F2.md](docs/REFEREE_RESPONSE_F2.md))
5. **The sign of dealer positions is the real open problem.** The reversal fits hedging theory only if Pro positions are the opposite of dealers', while the next-day volatility link fits only if they are the same. Estimating the sign from the market's response, the end-of-day outcome points to the reverse of Pro, the volatility outcome points nowhere clearly, and no estimated sign beats the fixed rules out of sample. ([DEALER_SIGN.md](docs/DEALER_SIGN.md))
6. **Pinning appears in one regime only.** The pre-stated test over all expiry days just misses 5% (p 0.054). Under the single Thursday weekly (41 expiry days), Nifty's official expiry close was 4.5 points closer to a 50-point strike than on other days (p = 0.0006, a subgroup test that does not survive the correction for all of the project's tests, 0.052); since the move to Tuesday there is none, and it is unrelated to dealer gamma. A test of six market-structure explanations, with its analysis plan committed before its results were computed, found that none explains when pinning comes and goes. ([PINNING_STUDY.md](docs/PINNING_STUDY.md), [PINNING_MECHANISM.md](docs/PINNING_MECHANISM.md))

For a quick look at the forecasts, one command draws any test day as a web page from committed files ([docs/DEMO.md](docs/DEMO.md)). I also froze a forecast for Tuesday 3 November's expiry in the repository before the market opened (a simple day-ahead volatility forecast and the option market's expected move, not the hybrid), so it can be scored against what actually happened ([results/live/2026-11-03/forecast.md](results/live/2026-11-03/forecast.md)).

## Points from your paper I would like to check with you

Offered as a careful reader's questions, each now checked on real data:

1. **The "before" window** (point 1 above).
2. **Connectedness scale.** Tables 3 and 4 report the sum of the two cross shares, which is twice the standard Diebold-Yilmaz index for two variables (84.00 corresponds to 42.00). Comparisons within the paper are unaffected. The overall fall in connectedness replicates on the inferred window (87.9 to 74.9 and 68.9 on your scale; printed 84.00 to 76.38 and 73.02), but not on the stated dates, where the before total is 73.1 and no fall is detected; the hour-level totals do not replicate on either window. A registered search over 5,440 settings per before window (normalisation, input series, horizon, VAR lag and how the hours are joined; P113) gets closest with a column-normalised decomposition of the log mcsGARCH variance, 5.3 points off on average, with 8 of the 15 totals within two points. No normalised (by row or by column) or Cholesky setting reaches the before last hour's 105.22 (the largest is 94.7): both of its printed cross shares are above one half, so each index would be explained more by the other's shocks than by its own. Only settings without any normalisation get there, and they then overshoot almost every other total. Under the best setting the fall on expiry days holds, but the much larger fall in the last hour does not. Were the shares normalised by row, and with which input, VAR lag and horizon? ([P113_CONNECTEDNESS_SETTINGS.md](docs/P113_CONNECTEDNESS_SETTINGS.md))
3. **Minute-level tests.** The Mann-Whitney tests in Tables 5 and 6 treat thousands of minutes from a few dozen days as independent. On real data the minute-level test gives p-values as small as 10^-256; tests on whole days keep the Nifty findings (higher volatility overall and on Thursdays) but not the Bank Nifty Wednesday result (+16%, not significant at the day level).
4. **Persistence.** Your Table 2 implies the half-life of an intraday volatility shock fell from about 339 to 72 minutes for Nifty. I find 359 to 72 on the inferred window, with a wide interval on the "before" side (197 to 1,255 minutes), so the fall is clear but its size is uncertain. Because mcsGARCH has no moving level inside the day, a drifting daily level can look like long-lived shocks, so I registered a test with three of the fixes of point 2 (today's level so far, the overnight fade and a HAR daily level; P114). For Nifty on the inferred window the fall survives by the registered rule (141 to 18 minutes, bootstrap p 0.022, though not after the correction for all of the project's tests), but not for Bank Nifty or on the stated or second-search windows, and across 44 rolling windows the late-2022 window is the most persistent of all. A second registered test compared the fall with 1,223 pairs of windows with the same lengths and gap that straddle neither of the two Bank Nifty moves inside the actual pair (P117). By its registered rule the fall is within the normal range (placebo p 0.19 under the model of your Table 2 and 0.11 under the fixed model, 0.21 after correcting for the two), and Fin Nifty, whose expiry calendar did not change, fell as much over the same days. For Nifty that verdict is weaker than it sounds: under the fixed model every placebo pair that fell as far either straddles a later calendar change (88 of 128) or contains a day of flat minute data from an NSE outage (40 of 128), and in the registered set with no calendar change at all, which has too few separate periods for a verdict, p is 0.06 (0.002 without the outage day, post hoc). Bank Nifty did have falls as large in calendar-stable years. So the test cannot tell an expiry effect from a market-wide change for Nifty; Fin Nifty's equal fall points to a market-wide change, but this is not settled, and only three or four separate periods stand behind the comparison. ([P114_PERSISTENCE_FIXED_MODEL.md](docs/P114_PERSISTENCE_FIXED_MODEL.md), [P117_PERSISTENCE_PLACEBO.md](docs/P117_PERSISTENCE_PLACEBO.md))
5. **A few printed numbers look like typesetting slips** (for example, Table 4's first-hour Nifty rows sum to 101, and one Anderson-Darling statistic in Table 1 is printed with a comma for the decimal point). A separate AI agent re-typed all 223 printed numbers blind from the PDF; 222 matched our first typing and the one difference is that comma, so these points are in the printed paper, not our transcription ([REKEY_CHECK.md](docs/REKEY_CHECK.md)).

## How to check any of this

Every one of the project's 28,515 hypothesis tests (37 studies) is listed in [results/test_ledger.csv](results/test_ledger.csv), summarised in [TEST_LEDGER.md](docs/TEST_LEDGER.md), with its raw p-value and a p-value corrected for all of them at once (Benjamini-Yekutieli). The hybrid's gain over mcsGARCH on all days survives that correction at every horizon, against my version and against your specification, and so do the Bank Nifty and Fin Nifty replications, the fixed mcsGARCH's gain over mcsGARCH (though its fixes were chosen after seeing the same days, so it awaits its forward test) and the Tuesday last-hour premium; the edge over an mcsGARCH given the implied variance does not, and the end-of-day reversal survives only in its in-sample form.

The code is a tested Python package. One command downloads the public data (pinned sources), checks every raw file against a list of SHA-256 hashes and reruns the main pipeline and the earlier studies (about 2 hours 20 minutes on one core when it was timed); each registered follow-up study gives its own commands in its document ([REPRODUCIBILITY.md](docs/REPRODUCIBILITY.md)). Each step that went wrong and how it was fixed is logged in [ITERATION_LOG.md](ITERATION_LOG.md), including two of my own headline claims that were withdrawn after a referee-style review and the closing-auction check ([INDEPENDENT_REVIEW.md](docs/INDEPENDENT_REVIEW.md)).

**How I did this.** I used AI assistance (Claude, by Anthropic) heavily for the code, the analyses and the write-ups, and the reviews and the blind re-key mentioned above were done by separate AI agents, not by other people. Every number in this pitch is listed in [number_registry.csv](docs/number_registry.csv) and checked by a script against the results file it comes from.
