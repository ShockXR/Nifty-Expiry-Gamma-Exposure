# Pitch: what dealer positioning adds to your expiry-day result

*For Prof. Piyush Pandey. From Shreyan Thanki, November 2026. Every result below is from **real NSE data**, 2 January 2023 to 2 November 2026 (942 trading days, 200 Nifty expiries), unless it says otherwise. Each one links to a document in this repository that shows the file it comes from.*

## The idea in three sentences

Your 2025 paper with Chhimwal and V. Pandey shows that each index became more volatile and more heavily traded on its own expiry day after NSE separated the Nifty and Bank Nifty expiries. I tested one candidate explanation, option dealers' gamma hedging, with public NSE files, first by asking whether dealer positioning improves out-of-sample forecasts beyond your mcsGARCH model, and then by trying hard to break every in-sample link I found. The answer is more interesting than a yes or a no, and it gives a concrete plan for a paper.

## What I found

1. **Your "after" sample replicates almost exactly, and I think I found your "before" window, to within about a week.** With 360 minutes a day I get exactly your 21,239 Nifty returns and Table 1's extremes to the printed digit; mcsGARCH gives alpha 0.0498 and beta 0.9407 (printed 0.0494 and 0.9410). The stated "before" period (November 2023 to February 2024) has 82 trading days, not 64, and does not match. Two independent searches of every 64-day window against Table 1 put it one year earlier, starting between 14 and 23 November 2022 and ending between 10 and 21 February 2023, with both printed "before" maxima reproduced to every decimal. The first search, which scores the spread and the tails, picks **14 November 2022 to 10 February 2023**; a second, registered search, run blind to the first by a separate AI agent, also uses your printed means and picks **22 November 2022 to 20 February 2023**, one of only two windows that reproduce both means. My first question to you is which dates you used. ([REPLICATION_NSE.md](docs/REPLICATION_NSE.md), [P108_BLIND_WINDOW.md](docs/P108_BLIND_WINDOW.md))
2. **At short horizons your model can be improved, and the improvement can be written as a few plain fixes to mcsGARCH.** Your paper fits mcsGARCH but does not forecast with it, so I ran an out-of-sample contest on 792 test days. A hybrid that keeps mcsGARCH and corrects its errors with a gradient-boosted model, using the previous evening's option chain, India VIX and calendar features, forecasts the next 15 and 30 minutes 4.4% and 6.6% better than mcsGARCH as your paper specifies it (QLIKE loss, Diebold-Mariano p below 0.001). My own implementation, which adds a separate intraday curve for expiry days and smooths the curves, turned out to forecast slightly worse than yours on Nifty (though better on Bank Nifty); against it the gain is 5.8% and 7.6%, and the studies below, run before I checked this, use it as the benchmark (P116). The hybrid's gain survives a correction for having tried 31 models and retraining with 20 new sets of random seeds. A registered ablation shows where the gain comes from: recent realized volatility (about a third) and the time of day (about a quarter). Removing the option chain, VIX or calendar inputs costs nothing, and a hybrid that sees only what mcsGARCH sees gets the whole gain (6.1% and 8.0%). A registered stress test on Bank Nifty and Fin Nifty with five sets of random seeds finds the same two sources and shows that the option chain and VIX are each worth less than 1% of mcsGARCH's loss; there the calendar inputs help a little and the mcsGARCH-information hybrid gets most, not all, of the gain (P110). A second registered study wrote down five fixes to mcsGARCH before computing any result, and together they make it 6.8% and 8.3% better than mcsGARCH at 15 and 30 minutes, 118% and 108% of the hybrid's gain (intervals 85% to 167% and 80% to 132%); the hybrid cannot be told apart from the fixed model (p 0.82 and 0.73). Three fixes do most of it: giving the minute-by-minute part today's level so far, one calibration factor per time of day, and letting yesterday's close fade overnight; a HAR daily level helps only inside the combination, and leverage does not help. The fixed model does not explain the last hour (13% of the hybrid's gain there), and on Bank Nifty and Fin Nifty, with nothing retuned, it recovers 97% to 136%. So the hybrid's gain is over mcsGARCH, not over a carefully fixed version of it; against your specification the fixed model is 5.5% and 7.3% better. Because the fixes were chosen after seeing these days, this explains the gain rather than proving a better model, and I registered a one-time forward test of them on Nifty days from 4 November 2026 (P112). Against the best of four mcsGARCH versions also given India VIX or option-implied variance, the hybrid is better by 4.8% and 5.9%, but that edge appears only after November 2024 and does not survive the correction for all of the project's tests. On point estimates it appears then mostly because that benchmark lost its advantage (82% to 87% of the change), although the benchmark's own change is not significant. The hybrid also replicates on Bank Nifty with nothing changed, under a plan committed before any Bank Nifty forecast was made (3.9% and 5.0% better on 660 test days), and a second registered replication on a third index, Fin Nifty, gives 4.9% and 6.1% on 604 test days, although there the last-hour gain (1.0%, p 0.30) and the expiry-day gain (Holm p 0.093) are not significant. Pooled in one registered test that allows for the three indices moving together, the gain is 4.7% and 6.0%, and the three count as about two independent replications, not three. In the last hour an mcsGARCH given VIX comes within 1.7% of the hybrid's 6.4% gain, plain gradient boosting does about as well as the hybrid, and under squared-error loss the models cannot be ranked. In money terms the gain is small: under a registered test, an investor who sizes a Nifty position by forecast volatility would pay only about 0.04% of wealth a year to switch from mcsGARCH to the hybrid, and the gain over the information-matched mcsGARCH is not significant. ([MODEL_ITERATIONS_REAL.md](docs/MODEL_ITERATIONS_REAL.md), [REFEREE_RESPONSE_FORECAST.md](docs/REFEREE_RESPONSE_FORECAST.md), [BANKNIFTY_FORECAST.md](docs/BANKNIFTY_FORECAST.md), [THIRD_INDEX_FORECAST.md](docs/THIRD_INDEX_FORECAST.md), [P107_POOLED_THREE_INDEX.md](docs/P107_POOLED_THREE_INDEX.md), [P106_GAIN_DECOMPOSITION.md](docs/P106_GAIN_DECOMPOSITION.md), [P109_MCSGARCH_EXTENSIONS.md](docs/P109_MCSGARCH_EXTENSIONS.md), [P103_ECONOMIC_VALUE.md](docs/P103_ECONOMIC_VALUE.md))
3. **The expiry effect lives at the end of the day, and part of it is noise.** Since Nifty's expiry moved to Tuesday (September 2025), Tuesday's last hour carries 63% more variance than ordinary days on which no NSE or BSE index option expires (26% more over the whole day). Before November 2024 almost every day was some index's expiry day, so no clean comparison exists for that period. When Bank Nifty's expiry moved from Thursday to Wednesday in 2023, its end-of-day variance moved with it (+0.90 log points in the last 30 minutes, with no baseline needed). Two cautions: end-of-day one-minute returns on expiry days reverse quickly, a sign of bid-ask noise, and a noise-robust estimator keeps only a half to three quarters of each premium; and the event-study placebo tests rest on at most four independent six-month windows, so none of those results reaches 5% under an honest small-sample test. A synthetic-control study of the November 2024 cut in weekly options was too imprecise to tell. ([REAL_DATA_RESULTS.md](docs/REAL_DATA_RESULTS.md), R2; [EVENT_STUDY.md](docs/EVENT_STUDY.md); [REFEREE_RESPONSE_EXPIRY.md](docs/REFEREE_RESPONSE_EXPIRY.md); [SYNTHETIC_CONTROL.md](docs/SYNTHETIC_CONTROL.md))
4. **Dealer gamma from public files does not help forecasts, and the one in-sample pattern that held up is about Pro positioning, not gamma.** GEX features add nothing out of sample on Nifty under any of three sign rules (on Bank Nifty a small gain at the 09:15 forecast needs its own test, and it did not reappear on Fin Nifty). In sample, Nifty reverses into the close (about 3 bp per 100 bp move over the day), which already counts against the simplest hedging story, since dealers short all options would make the close continue the day's move. The reversal appears only after evenings when NSE's "Pro" participants were net short index options (equivalently, Pro-based GEX was negative: the two agree on 916 of 932 evenings, and weighting by gamma adds nothing). It survived block shuffles, timing placebos, random sign rules, a split-sample test and a Holm correction over the 78 headline tests (p = 0.016), but not a correction over the full family of 742 tests. The negative-Pro evenings come in long runs (95% of 2023 evenings, about one in five from 2025), so the honest sample is a few dozen episodes; with inference built for that, the pattern holds in the full sample and from 2025 but not inside 2023 to 2024. As a trade it earns about 4 bp before costs and about zero after them (break-even slippage about 0.55 bp per side). ([FALSIFICATION.md](docs/FALSIFICATION.md), [F2_ECONOMICS.md](docs/F2_ECONOMICS.md), [REFEREE_RESPONSE_F2.md](docs/REFEREE_RESPONSE_F2.md))
5. **The sign of dealer positions is the real open problem.** The reversal fits hedging theory only if Pro positions are the opposite of dealers', while the next-day volatility link fits only if they are the same. Estimating the sign from the market's response, the end-of-day outcome points to the reverse of Pro, the volatility outcome points nowhere clearly, and no estimated sign beats the fixed rules out of sample. ([DEALER_SIGN.md](docs/DEALER_SIGN.md))
6. **Pinning is real but regime-specific.** Under the single Thursday weekly, Nifty's official expiry close was 4.5 points closer to a 50-point strike than on other days (p = 0.0006); since the move to Tuesday there is none, and it is unrelated to dealer gamma. A test of six market-structure explanations, with its analysis plan committed before its results were computed, found that none explains when pinning comes and goes. ([PINNING_STUDY.md](docs/PINNING_STUDY.md), [PINNING_MECHANISM.md](docs/PINNING_MECHANISM.md))

For a quick look at the forecasts, one command draws any test day as a web page from committed files ([docs/DEMO.md](docs/DEMO.md)). I also froze a forecast for Tuesday 3 November's expiry in the repository before the market opened, so it can be scored against what actually happened ([results/live/2026-11-03/forecast.md](results/live/2026-11-03/forecast.md)).

## Points from your paper I would like to check with you

Offered as a careful reader's questions, each now checked on real data:

1. **The "before" window** (point 1 above).
2. **Connectedness scale.** Tables 3 and 4 report the sum of the two cross shares, which is twice the standard Diebold-Yilmaz index for two variables (84.00 corresponds to 42.00). Comparisons within the paper are unaffected. The overall fall in connectedness replicates on the inferred window (87.9 to 74.9 and 68.9 on your scale; printed 84.00 to 76.38 and 73.02), but not on the stated dates, where the before total is 73.1 and no fall is detected; the hour-level totals do not replicate on either window. A registered search over 5,440 settings per before window (normalisation, input series, horizon, VAR lag and how the hours are joined; P113) gets closest with a column-normalised decomposition of the log mcsGARCH variance, 5.3 points off on average, with 8 of the 15 totals within two points. No normalised (by row or by column) or Cholesky setting reaches the before last hour's 105.22 (the largest is 94.7): both of its printed cross shares are above one half, so each index would be explained more by the other's shocks than by its own. Only settings without any normalisation get there, and they then overshoot almost every other total. Under the best setting the fall on expiry days holds, but the much larger fall in the last hour does not. Were the shares normalised by row, and with which input, VAR lag and horizon? ([P113_CONNECTEDNESS_SETTINGS.md](docs/P113_CONNECTEDNESS_SETTINGS.md))
3. **Minute-level tests.** The Mann-Whitney tests in Tables 5 and 6 treat thousands of minutes from a few dozen days as independent. On real data the minute-level test gives p-values as small as 10^-256; tests on whole days keep the Nifty findings (higher volatility overall and on Thursdays) but not the Bank Nifty Wednesday result (+16%, not significant at the day level).
4. **Persistence.** Your Table 2 implies the half-life of an intraday volatility shock fell from about 339 to 72 minutes for Nifty. I find 359 to 72 on the inferred window, with a wide interval on the "before" side (197 to 1,255 minutes), so the fall is clear but its size is uncertain. Because mcsGARCH has no moving level inside the day, a drifting daily level can look like long-lived shocks, so I registered a test with the fixed model of point 2 (P114). For Nifty on the inferred window the fall survives (141 to 18 minutes, bootstrap p 0.022), but not for Bank Nifty or on the stated or second-search windows, and across 44 rolling windows the late-2022 window is the most persistent of all. So the fall may partly be a return from an unusually persistent period rather than an effect of the expiry change. ([P114_PERSISTENCE_FIXED_MODEL.md](docs/P114_PERSISTENCE_FIXED_MODEL.md))
5. **A few printed numbers look like typesetting slips** (for example, Table 4's first-hour Nifty rows sum to 101, and one Anderson-Darling statistic in Table 1 is printed with a comma for the decimal point). A separate AI agent re-typed all 223 printed numbers blind from the PDF; 222 matched our first typing and the one difference is that comma, so these points are in the printed paper, not our transcription ([REKEY_CHECK.md](docs/REKEY_CHECK.md)).

## What I would ask for

* **Your view on the "Pro" category** in NSE's participant-wise open interest as a proxy for dealers. My results suggest it is not a clean one, and this is where your knowledge of the Indian market would matter most.
* **Data:** the exact "before" sample dates, and whether your group has intraday option-chain snapshots (positions during the day, not only at the close), which would let me test the hedging story directly on expiry afternoons.

## How to check any of this

Every one of the project's 28,414 hypothesis tests (35 studies) is listed in [TEST_LEDGER.md](docs/TEST_LEDGER.md) with its raw p-value and a p-value corrected for all of them at once (Benjamini-Yekutieli). The hybrid's gain over mcsGARCH on all days survives that correction at every horizon, and so do the Bank Nifty and Fin Nifty replications, the fixed mcsGARCH's gain over mcsGARCH (though its fixes were chosen after seeing the same days, so it awaits its forward test) and the Tuesday last-hour premium; the edge over an mcsGARCH given the implied variance does not, and the end-of-day reversal survives only in its in-sample form.

The code is a tested Python package with one command that downloads the public data (pinned sources), checks every raw file against a list of SHA-256 hashes and reruns every study ([REPRODUCIBILITY.md](docs/REPRODUCIBILITY.md)). Each step that went wrong and how it was fixed is logged in [ITERATION_LOG.md](ITERATION_LOG.md), including two of my own headline claims that were withdrawn after a referee-style review and the closing-auction check ([INDEPENDENT_REVIEW.md](docs/INDEPENDENT_REVIEW.md)).

**How I did this.** I used AI assistance (Claude, by Anthropic) heavily for the code, the analyses and the write-ups, and the reviews and the blind re-key mentioned above were done by separate AI agents, not by other people. I have checked that every number traces to code and data in the repository, and the whole project reruns with one command.
